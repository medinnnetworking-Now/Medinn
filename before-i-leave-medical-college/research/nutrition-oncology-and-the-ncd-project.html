A cross-sectional study is a snapshot

You study a population at one point in time. It gives you prevalence, not cause. This is how Dr. Jerin James and I did one in medical college: the steps, the guide beside each step, and why it never became a paper.

Why we did it

Dr. Jerin James and I put a lot of time into working on this study. We did it because we felt it was a worthy enough research topic and it deserved our attention. And because the opportunity presented itself in the form of a health camp, where we would not otherwise get the data.

We went through every step: the protocol, the study design, collecting a humongous amount of data, and then trying to analyse it. We could not crunch a paper out of it. It took a lot of our time in college, and we did not give an initial orientation, even to our guide, on how to go about structuring a research paper.

Narrow the topic first

For the cross-sectional study, the work was in narrowing down the research topic. Who we were studying, how we were going to study them, what guidelines or parent studies already existed, and finding an appropriate tool we could administer to get the data we needed.

  1. WhoDefine the population: which people, where, and how will you select them?
  2. HowChoose the design and the setting.
  3. Parent studies and guidelinesFind existing surveys and frameworks to build on, so you do not start from zero.
  4. A toolA validated questionnaire or instrument you can actually administer.

I decided that I was going to study non-communicable diseases. So I started searching for templates that had the questionnaires and everything one might need to conduct the study.

Finding WHO STEPS

That is how I stumbled upon the WHO STEPS protocol, and I started making the questionnaire. I prepared the research protocol, and I was clueless as to how to collect the data, because WHO STEPS requires both biochemical data and anthropometric data.

  1. WHO STEPSThe WHO STEPwise approach to NCD risk factor surveillance: a questionnaire, physical measurements and biochemical tests.
  2. AnthropometryHeight, weight, waist circumference.
  3. Biochemical dataBlood tests, which you cannot collect from a questionnaire alone.

Then, by coincidence, a health camp

Nearing third year, due to several setbacks, I was in a bout of depression, on medication and so forth. But yet, I saw an opportunity manifest before my eyes.

Very coincidentally, the then College Union had decided to run a health camp. A lot of data would come in, a lot of tests would be done. Why waste it if we could assess the health metrics and risk factors of all the students on campus?

The Institutional Research Committee

We submitted our protocol to the IRC, the Institutional Research Committee. The committee reviews it, and the head of the IRC gave me corrections. I did the corrections, and after certain periodic reviews we were cleared for the IEC.

  1. Submit the protocolFull protocol to the Institutional Research Committee.
  2. Take the correctionsThe reviewer sends comments. Answer each one.
  3. Periodic reviewResubmit until you are cleared.
  4. Cleared for the IECOnly then do you go to the ethics committee.

The Institutional Ethics Committee

We took a lot of printouts and submitted a request to the IEC. Then I went to the Institutional Ethics Committee meeting, where all the people on the committee reviewed the proposal, and our paper was ready for the Institutional Ethics Committee.

The study, and the mountain of data

So we did the study, and we were sitting on a mountain of data. We would go to each health camp, guide students through the camp, collect the data and measure their physical parameters, and we had the help of the whole college in doing so.

The analysis we were not ready for

We were unprepared as to how to analyse the data. We did not have ChatGPT or Claude at the time, so we worked manually, with SPSS, Excel and Google Sheets. We were very clueless, and depended heavily on Dr. Ashik, who taught us SPSS at every point.

It took another one and a half years to get into the data, understand it and learn how to process it using SPSS and Excel formulas to break it down set by set. That took a lot of our time in the final year, particularly the eighth semester.

We eventually managed to prepare a report and present it at national forums and undergraduate conferences in college. But we could never get around to publishing it because of the vast amount of data. We did have a guide who had published, but we did not orient the project with them well enough on how to go about structuring and handling it.

To this day, I believe we spread ourselves too thin.

Too much data. Far too much data. Unsegmented, without the necessary tools or the team to understand the data, structure it properly or even publish it.

The project eventually earned me the KUHS UG Research Award, one of the first from my college. Dr. Jerin James and I spent the most time on it, and he is one of the reasons we were able to submit the report for the KUHS UG Research Award. But to my dismay, we were never able to publish it. That is something that will always bother me, and something I owe the people who made it possible, and the campus, for a while.

If we did it again

  1. One questionNot every risk factor at once.
  2. A paper planKnow the tables and the target journal before you collect.
  3. Orient your guideWe had one who had published. Brief them on the plan before you collect.
  4. A team for the dataSplit the analysis early.
