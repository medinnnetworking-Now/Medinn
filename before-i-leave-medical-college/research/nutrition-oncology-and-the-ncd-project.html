The First Research Questions: Nutrition, Oncology and the NCD Project

Sidharth Narayanan, MBBS iD
Government Medical College, Kozhikode
I won a research award for a project I could never publish.

BACKGROUND: In second year, I went department by department, ward by ward, trying to understand oncology and nutrition and what was being done.

METHODS: With Institutional Research Committee and Ethics Committee approval, we drafted a protocol based on the WHO STEPS 1 and 2 programme to assess risk factors for non-communicable diseases on campus.

RESULTS: We prepared a report and presented it at national forums and undergraduate conferences. The project earned me the KUHS UG Research Award.

CONCLUSIONS: We could never get around to publishing it. Far too much data.

Second year: oncology and nutrition

While in second year, after certain setbacks and things that I never anticipated the way they would go, I took a few steps backward and reanalysed what I might have always wanted to do and what I wanted to focus on.

I started going department by department, ward by ward, trying to understand oncology and nutrition and what was being done.

I tried to understand if there was a nutritionist on board, why the diet books were always empty, and why the diet books were always filled up with things on renal-related diets and the diet was always just milk and eggs.

Then I realised how a state like ours could only provide the set means, how protein is important, and how that is what the government is trying to do.

But then again, I felt there was an imperative need to understand nutrition, assess the nutritional status of patients and their recovery, and understand the oncological status and so on.

So I started reading.

I started going into journals. I started looking up random terms in oncology, trying to understand what a review of literature was and what those nuances were.

I started talking to radiation oncologists. They told me to read DeVita and Long’s oncology textbooks, which I did not particularly like, but yet I looked up how colon cancer and meat intake were related and how certain substances were classified as carcinogens.

So who was assessing whether the people in Malabar were getting higher cancer rates due to this very rate of consumption?

This was me in second year.

For a while, I was focusing on these topics. I was trying to make a research project out of it, but I didn’t understand where to go or where to begin.

I would talk to a professor, then to a JR, and they started writing it down.

My dear professor and HOD, Dr. Asma Rahim, and Dr. Ashik Refeek told me to narrow it down.

We started making it into nutritional assessment of patients, and then we modified it to cancer patients.

I took this very project to my Medical Oncology professor, Dr. Sanudev. He told me we could do it, and then I let go of that project for a while.

The NCD project

It was third year. The academic pressure was getting to me. The conventional academic pressure. And I was never always just studying. I was always doing a couple of things at the same time, getting into politics, literature and everything that consumed me whole.

Nearing third year, due to several setbacks, it was a difficult stretch. But yet, I saw an opportunity manifest before my eyes.

It was an initiative by the then College Union to run a health camp.

A lot of things came back to me, and I started thinking: a lot of data would come in, a lot of tests would be done. Why waste it if we could assess the health metrics and risk factors of all the students on campus?

And that was how we drafted a protocol based on the WHO STEPS 1 and 2 programme, where we assess the risk factors for NCDs.

I drafted a proposal based on that, made the protocol, and we decided to collect the data and carry out a STEPS 1 and 2 programme for assessing the risk factors for non-communicable diseases.

While this happened, we collected data in an unconventional manner, trying to sort through it and make sense of it, with Institutional Research Committee approval and Ethics Committee approval.

Soon after, it took another one and a half years for me to get into the data, understand it and learn how to process it using SPSS and Excel formulas to segregate the data and break it down set by set.

That took a lot of my time in the final year, particularly the eighth semester.

We eventually managed to prepare a report and present it at national forums and undergraduate conferences in college.

But we could never get around to publishing it because of the vast amount of data and the lack of mentorship and guidance on how to go about handling it.

To this day, I believe we spread ourselves too thin.

Too much data. Far too much data. Unsegmented, without the necessary tools or the team to understand the data, structure it properly or even publish it.

The project eventually earned me the KUHS UG Research Award.

But to my dismay, I was never able to publish it.

That is something that will always bother me. And it is something that I will owe the people who made it possible, and the campus, for a while.
