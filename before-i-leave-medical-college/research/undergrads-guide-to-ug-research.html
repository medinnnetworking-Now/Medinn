Most people do it for the portfolio. I did it for the story.

A lot of people get into undergraduate research to go abroad, write the USMLE, or clear PLAB or AMC. I got into it purely because I loved reading, building on it and building a narrative.

This is the guide I wish I had had: how I got in, the studies I did, what worked, and what I got wrong. It covers a cross-sectional study, a case report, electives, a peer review of an online preprint, and a 25-year retrospective analysis that got into the Journal of the American Heart Association. Each part has its own full article. Here is the whole arc in one place.

1. How you actually get in: see a problem.

It started before medical college, with a person who believed in me and talked to me about evidence-based medicine, plant-based diets, and how food can fundamentally change healthcare. He gave me books, including How Not to Die. I was skeptical of some of what I saw and read, and that was the first lesson: separate the claim from the data. I decided to repeat for MBBS. Then I joined college and lost track of that spark. Midway in first year he sent me the book he always talked about, and I started thinking about research again.

  1. ObserveA problem you saw, on a ward, in a clinic, on campus.
  2. AskTurn it into one answerable research question.
  3. Review the literatureSearch PubMed and Google Scholar, read abstracts, then full texts. Find the gap.
  4. Find a guideIf you are inside an institution, a faculty member in the right department who agrees to guide you.

2. The cross-sectional study: a mountain of data, no paper.

Dr. Jerin James and I were two hungry undergrads who knew nothing. We studied risk factors for non-communicable diseases among medical students, using the WHO STEPS protocol, and the opportunity came as a health camp run by the College Union, where we would not otherwise have got the data.

  1. Narrow the topicWho, how, which parent studies and guidelines, which tool.
  2. Find a frameworkWe found the WHO STEPS protocol and built the questionnaire.
  3. Write the protocolIt needed anthropometric and biochemical data too.
  4. Clear the Institutional Research CommitteeTake the corrections, resubmit, wait for clearance.
  5. Clear the Institutional Ethics CommitteePrintouts, a request, and a meeting.
  6. Collect, then analyseWe worked in SPSS, Excel and Google Sheets, and depended on Dr. Ashik to teach us SPSS.

It earned us the KUHS UG Research Award, one of the first from our college. We never published it. We had a guide who had published, but we did not orient the project with them well enough, and we spread ourselves too thin. That study is why we realised we needed a forum to understand research and actually learn the basics, which is the idea behind CMC Analytica.

3. The electives: one posting, several projects.

Our electives were a rare chance to choose a topic. In Community Medicine, our HOD and preceptor let us explore juvenile justice. We obtained permissions from magistrates, attended hearings, spoke to NGOs and social workers, and saw case files through the appropriate channels. We presented it and made a report, but because the data involved children, we chose not to publish. Not everything should be published.

In Medical Oncology, the topic I had once discussed with my professor, nutrition and cancer, came back as the elective topic. We learnt nutritional assessment tools and how to ask patients the right questions, and I saw that nobody was helping patients with their diet, before or after diagnosis. That posting produced a nutritional assessment study and a case report. None of it was a solo effort: my collaborators in Medical Oncology were Dr. Varun, Dr. Punya and Dr. Shadi, with our guide Dr. Sanudev, and in juvenile justice Dr. Varsha, Raniya, Shana and Sreelakshmi S.

4. The case report: fairly easy, if done properly.

There are always rare cases in a big tertiary care hospital. Our guide, Dr. Sanudev, told us about an interesting one. It was mostly retrospective: we took consent from the patient at follow-up, collected the old records, the histopathology slides and the imaging, and had next-generation sequencing done. We reviewed existing cases to show what made ours stand out, and wrote and rewrote. The first submission was rejected. We corrected it, resubmitted, and answered the reviewers one by one. It took almost a year to be published.

  1. Find the caseThrough a guide, or on the wards.
  2. Get written consentFrom the patient, usually at follow-up.
  3. Collect what existsRecords, slides, imaging, any tests you add.
  4. Follow CAREThe international guideline for case reports. Cite it.
  5. Expect rejectionCorrect, resubmit, answer every reviewer comment.

5. The online preprint review: an amusing start.

My first published credit was not a study. It was a peer review of an online preprint, "Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare: 2023 Year in Review", in JMIRx Med, a virtual collaborative live review discussion, in September 2024. It is funny, and it is also a real way in: you learn to read a paper critically, and you get your name on something published.

  1. Reviewing teaches you to readYou see how papers are built, and where they are weak.
  2. It is open to undergradsLook for collaborative or community reviews of preprints.
  3. Credit is realCheck how contributors are listed, and cite it accurately.

6. The 25-year retrospective analysis that got into JAHA.

Before my final-year examinations, a senior who had been doing meta-analyses and systematic reviews started an international forum to guide students around the world. The idea was to work together, remotely, on publicly available datasets and problem statements. Trying to tick every box in the undergraduate research journey, I joined an analysis using the CDC WONDER database.

It was hard. I did not own a laptop, my internet was poor, and I was studying for final-year MBBS examinations. I got the datasets wrong at first, I screwed up the analysis, then did it again, and my co-authors eventually helped me finish it. Midway through my house surgency, the paper was published: "Rising Obesity-Driven Cardiac Mortality Among Young Adults in the United States: A 25-Year Retrospective Analysis (1999 to 2023)", in the Journal of the American Heart Association, October 2026 (DOI 10.1161/JAHA.126.049462). I am the fourth author, and the analysis used CDC WONDER multiple-cause-of-death data for ages 15 to 44.

  1. Use public dataOpen datasets such as CDC WONDER are free to use. Check the source's terms and your journal's requirements.
  2. Work remotely, with a teamCo-authors across the world can fix what you get wrong.
  3. Redo itWrong datasets and wrong analyses happen. Check, then rerun.
  4. Be honest about your roleI am the fourth author on this paper.

What I wish I had known

  1. Start from a problem you sawThen one question, then the literature, then a guide.
  2. Orient your guideBrief them on the plan before you collect, not after.
  3. Plan the analysis firstDraw the empty tables, know the software, and plan the paper.
  4. Approvals before dataThe Institutional Research Committee, then the Institutional Ethics Committee.
  5. Find collaboratorsCredit them, publicly.
  6. Publish what you canProtect what you must, such as data about children.
  7. Expect rejectionCorrect, resubmit, answer every comment.
  8. Keep the question smallOne question beats a mountain of data.

Undergraduate research should not just be about collecting publications and certificates. It should be about understanding problems, building a scientific temperament, learning how to question evidence, and finding ways to solve those problems.
