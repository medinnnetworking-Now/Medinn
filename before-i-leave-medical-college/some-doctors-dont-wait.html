Some doctors don’t wait. They build.

Dr. Maanas Jain is the founder of Quintubate. He made a video-laryngoscope that attaches to the laryngoscope a hospital already owns. I asked him what it takes to build medical devices for Indian healthcare.

Dr. Sidharth NarayananHouse Surgeon, GMC Kozhikode
November 2026 · 6 min read

Red a critiqueBlack an insightBlue the evidence

MBBS is the foundation of every doctor’s career. Right now, it feels undervalued. Part of what we owe it is to ask what that foundation can lead to.

So before I leave medical college, I want to tell you about Quintubate, and about a doctor who did not wait.

Who he is

Dr. Maanas Jain’s profile lists MBBS at AIIMS Jodhpur and Internal Medicine at Rutgers University in the United States[1]. Before starting residency, he set out to build Quintubate. He is its founder and CTO.

A cost that stands in the way

A standard laryngoscope costs roughly ₹1,000 to 2,000. In Dr. Jain’s own video, a video-laryngoscope is shown at ₹2,00,000 to ₹3,00,000[2]. A video view of the airway helps the person intubating, the person learning, and the record. It also sits out of reach of many hospitals.

What he built

UVEAL attaches to existing laryngoscopes and turns them into video-laryngoscopes that send the view to a smartphone[3]. The company describes it as CDSCO certified. Dr. Jain says that in six months since commercialization, UVEAL is being used by 250+ doctors across India and 12 other countries. Those figures are his, not independently verified. His video shows a real intubation at Civil Hospital, Gandhinagar.

In his words

What are you building?

“Quintubate is an initiative focussed on democratizing access to high-tech, expensive medical devices by manufacturing and developing existing, as well as novel, crucially important medical devices at a sustainable cost for Indian healthcare.”

What are the bottlenecks you face?

“The medical device ecosystem is largely limited to a select few companies, all based internationally, and hence healthcare in India is not supportive of made-in-India devices. Clinicians are not aware, and are skeptical of Indian medical device startups.”

What bridges need to be built?

“There needs to be an ecosystem for hospitals to learn about new licensed products developed by medical device startups as well as an incentive to adopt such indigenously designed devices. Regulators, and medical colleges need to be on the lookout and supportive to push for the adoption of such devices to promote a culture of development.”

How can we increase adoption and discovery?

“Fostering dialogue between medical device startups and existing hospitals, and having the opportunity to present the work to them would greatly enhance the chances of adoption.”

How do you see the future of healthcare?

“The trend of medical devices is towards consolidation of all high-tech products in a select few companies, none of which are based in India. If Indian [doctors and hospitals] do not push for development and adoption of indigenous devices, medical device innovation in India will inevitably stagnate.”

What needs to be done?

“There is a big opportunity. Since very few medical devices are actually made in India, there is a huge scope for founders to pitch in and innovate to fill that large gap.”

What he needs now

Meetings with hospitals and administrators, to explain the device and explore how it can be procured and used. Access to grants for new prototype development. Introductions to hospital administrators and government healthcare officials.

What this says about MBBS

A doctor sees a problem at the bedside. What happens next depends on whether anyone makes room for the solution.

MBBS is where doctors learn to see what needs fixing. The next step needs hospitals willing to look, grants, and a culture that adopts what is built here. Strengthen the foundation first. Then, for those who want to build on it, make the way possible.

If we do not, the doctors who see the problem will keep leaving the solving to someone else.
