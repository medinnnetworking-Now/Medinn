STEP 1 · THE DESIGN

A cross-sectional study is a snapshot.

You look at a population at one point in time. You measure risk factors and conditions together.
It tells you how common something is. It cannot tell you what caused what.

STEP 2 · THE TOOL

Do not invent your questions. Use a framework.

1
QuestionnaireTobacco, alcohol, diet, physical activity
2
Physical measurementsHeight, weight, waist, blood pressure
3
Biochemical testsBlood glucose, cholesterol
We ran Steps 1 and 2.
SOURCE: WHO STEPS, the stepwise approach to NCD risk factor surveillance.

STEP 3 · THE PROTOCOL

Draft the protocol first.

1
Question and objectivesWhat exactly are you asking?
2
Design, setting, populationWho, where, and how many?
3
Tools and variablesWhat will you measure, and how?
4
Analysis planWhich tables will the paper need?
5
Ethics and consentWho approves it, and how do you take consent?

STEP 4 · APPROVALS

No data before approval.

Take the protocol to the Institutional Research Committee and the Ethics Committee. We had both before we collected anything.
Approval comes before the first participant, not after the first spreadsheet.
Dr. Sidharth Narayanan · House Surgeon, GMC Kozhikode05 / 12

STEP 5 · THE ANALYSIS PLAN

Plan the analysis before the data.

Decide your variables, how you will code them, and what the empty tables look like. Draw the tables before you collect a single row.
I did not do this. That is the part of the story that cost me.

THE OPPORTUNITY

A health camp. Why waste the data?

The then College Union was running a health camp. A lot of tests would be done.
Why not assess the risk factors of all the students on campus? That is how we drafted the WHO STEPS 1 and 2 protocol.

WHAT WENT WRONG

We collected first. We understood later.

It took another one and a half years to get into the data, and learn to process it with SPSS and Excel.
That took a lot of my time in the final year, particularly the eighth semester.
Dr. Sidharth Narayanan · House Surgeon, GMC Kozhikode09 / 12

THE MISTAKE

We spread ourselves too thin.

Too much data. Far too much data. Unsegmented, without the tools or the team to understand it, structure it properly or even publish it.

DO IT BETTER

If I did it again:

1
One questionNot every risk factor at once.
2
Fewer variablesOnly what you will analyse.
3
Analysis plan firstEmpty tables before data.
4
A mentor who has publishedBefore you collect, not after.
