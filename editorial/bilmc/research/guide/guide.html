PART A · THE CROSS-SECTIONAL STUDY

A snapshot of a group.

You study a population at one point in time. It tells you how common something is (prevalence). It cannot tell you what caused what.

It is good for

  • Prevalence of a condition or risk factor
  • Describing a population
  • Planning services and screening
  • Generating questions for later studies

It cannot

  • Show what came first, the exposure or the outcome
  • Prove cause
  • Follow people over time

OUR STUDY

Risk factors for non-communicable diseases among medical students in a college in North Kerala. We used the WHO STEPS protocol and collected data at the college health camps. It earned us the KUHS UG Research Award. We never published it. The next four pages show every step, and the mistakes.

PART A · STEPS 1 TO 4

From topic to opportunity.

1
Narrow down the topic

Decide who you are studying, how you will study them, which guidelines or parent studies already exist, and which tool you can actually administer.

For us, the work was in narrowing the topic. We chose non-communicable diseases.
2
Find a framework and a tool

Look for existing templates with ready questionnaires. A validated tool beats one you invent.

We searched for templates and stumbled upon the WHO STEPS protocol. We started building the questionnaire.
3
Write the protocol

Question and objectives, population and sample, tools and variables, analysis plan, ethics. Page 6 has a skeleton.

Our protocol was ready. But WHO STEPS needs anthropometric and biochemical data too, and we were clueless about how to collect it.
4
Find the data opportunity

Look for events or settings where the data is already being collected.

The then College Union decided to run a health camp. A lot of tests would be done. Why waste that?
Reference: WHO. The WHO STEPwise approach to noncommunicable disease risk factor surveillance (STEPS). who.int

PART A · STEPS 5 TO 8

Approvals, data, analysis.

5
Clear the Institutional Research Committee

Submit the full protocol. Expect corrections. Answer each one and resubmit until you are cleared for the ethics committee.

The head of the IRC sent corrections. We made them. After periodic reviews, we were cleared for the IEC.
6
Clear the Institutional Ethics Committee

Submit a request with the printouts the committee asks for, and attend the meeting where members review the proposal. Do not collect data before approval.

We took a lot of printouts and submitted a request to the IEC. I went to the meeting where all members reviewed our proposal.
7
Collect the data

Plan who collects what, where and when. Keep records tidy from the first participant.

We went to each health camp, guided students through it and measured their physical parameters, with the help of the whole college. We ended up with a mountain of data.
8
Analyse it

Plan the analysis before you collect. Know the software. Find someone who can teach it.

We had no ChatGPT or Claude then. We worked in SPSS, Excel and Google Sheets, and depended on Dr. Ashik, who taught us SPSS at every point. It took another year and a half.
Reference: ICMR. National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants, 2017.

We collected a lot, and planned too little for the analysis. A smaller dataset with a clear plan beats a mountain you cannot structure.

PART A · REPORTING

Write it up with STROBE.

STROBE is the international reporting guideline for observational studies, including cross-sectional ones. Journals expect you to follow it and often ask for the checklist.

In our own words, a cross-sectional paper should cover:

  • Title and abstract: say it is a cross-sectional study, and summarise what you did and found.
  • Introduction: the background, and your objectives.
  • Methods: design, setting, participants, variables, data sources and tools, ways you handled bias, how you chose the sample size, and the statistical methods.
  • Results: how many took part, who they were, the main results, and any extra analyses.
  • Discussion: key results, limitations, how to interpret them, and whether they apply to others.
  • Other: funding.

USE THE OFFICIAL CHECKLIST

This is a summary. The 22-item checklist is on the STROBE website, with versions for cross-sectional, cohort and case-control studies. The authors hold the copyright, so download it from the source and cite them.

Reference: von Elm E, Altman DG, Egger M, Pocock SJ, Gotzsche PC, Vandenbroucke JP. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement: guidelines for reporting observational studies. BMJ 2007;335:806-808. strobe-statement.org

What we got wrong, in one line

We had the data and the approvals. We did not have a paper plan, or a briefing for our guide on how to structure it. We did have a guide who had published. We did not orient the project with them well enough.

PART B · THE CASE REPORT

One patient, told well.

A case report is the detailed account of one patient, usually rare or instructive. It is the easier design to start with, if you have a guide and a case.

1
Find the case

Rare cases turn up in any big tertiary care hospital. Ask a guide who knows an interesting one.

Our guide, Dr. Sanudev, told us about an interesting case.
2
Get consent

Talk to the patient, usually at follow-up, and take written consent for publishing their details.

We got consent by talking to the patient as they came for follow-up.
3
Collect what exists

Most case reports are retrospective. Gather the old records, the pathology slides and blocks, and the imaging.

We collected the histopathology slides and radiology images, and the old patient records. We also had next-generation sequencing (NGS) done.
4
Tabulate and review the literature

Put the data together. Review existing cases. What makes yours different, and what makes it stand out?

We reviewed existing cases and wrote and rewrote the paper many times.
5
Submit, and expect rejection

Choose a journal that publishes case reports. If it is rejected, make the corrections and send it again. Answer every reviewer comment, one by one.

The first time around, it was rejected. We corrected it and sent it again. We answered the reviewers one by one. It took almost a year to be published.
Our case report: Indian J Med Paediatr Oncol, April 2025. A metastatic solitary fibrous tumour of the parapharyngeal region, with review of literature.

PART B · REPORTING

Structure it with CARE.

CARE is the international guideline for reporting case reports. These are its section headings, with a one-line note in our own words.

1
Title. Say it is a case report and name the key feature.
2
Key words. A few terms that help people find it.
3
Abstract. Introduction, the case, and the main takeaway.
4
Introduction. Why this case is worth reporting, with references.
5
Patient information. Who the patient is, main concerns, relevant history.
6
Clinical findings. What you found on examination.
7
Timeline. The sequence of events, ideally as a figure or table.
8
Diagnostic assessment. Tests done, challenges, reasoning.
9
Therapeutic intervention. What was done, how, and any changes.
10
Follow-up and outcomes. How the patient did, results, any unexpected events.
11
Discussion. Strengths and limitations, the literature, your reasoning, the takeaway.
12
Patient perspective. What the patient shared, where appropriate.
13
Informed consent. Confirmation that consent was obtained.
Reference: Gagnier JJ, Kienle G, Altman DG, Moher D, Sox H, Riley D; the CARE Group. The CARE Guidelines: Consensus-based Clinical Case Reporting Guideline Development. BMJ Case Rep 2013;2013:bcr2013201554. Explanation and Elaboration: J Clin Epidemiol 2017. Please cite them, and use the official checklist from care-statement.org or the EQUATOR Network.

ONE-PAGE CHECKLISTS

Before you start.

Cross-sectional study

  • One clear question and objective
  • A guide who has published, briefed on the plan
  • A framework or validated tool
  • Protocol written, with sample size and sampling
  • Tables drawn before data collection
  • Institutional Research Committee cleared
  • Institutional Ethics Committee cleared
  • Consent process ready
  • Software and analysis owner decided
  • STROBE checklist downloaded

Case report

  • A rare or instructive case
  • A guide who knows the case
  • Written consent from the patient
  • Records, slides and imaging collected
  • Timeline drawn
  • Literature review done
  • What makes this case different, stated
  • CARE checklist followed
  • Journal chosen
  • Ready for rejection and reviewer comments

IF YOU ARE INSIDE AN INSTITUTION

Find a guide: a faculty member in the right department who agrees to guide your work, and brief them early on the plan and the paper. If you are outside an institution, the next article in the series covers what to do.

REMEMBER

  • A small question beats a mountain of data.
  • Plan the analysis, and the paper, before you collect.
  • Rejection is part of it. Correct, and resubmit.
