# Cancer care in India: scattered, and still not reaching the community
*A Medinn workup, prompted by a People of Healthcare conversation with Dr. Moni Abraham Kuriakose, Karkinos Healthcare*

Status: DRAFT. Statistics come from public sources listed at the end, several of which are secondary or non-peer-reviewed; verify each against the primary paper or official report before publishing. "Scattered at best" is Medinn's reading of the evidence, not a measured quantity.

## The short version
India has well over a million new cancer cases a year. Most are found late, the specialist centres that can treat them are concentrated in a few places, and many patients drop out of the system after treatment, when follow-up matters most. Dr. Kuriakose's answers point at the same gap from the inside: the model is built around big hospitals, and care has not been designed to flow back out to the community.

## 1. The burden
- GLOBOCAN 2022 puts India at about 1.41 million new cancer cases (1,413,316) for 2022 (via Down To Earth).
- NCRP-based estimates are slightly higher: about 1.46 million cases in 2022, and a 2025 JAMA Network Open study projects about 1.56 million cases and 874,404 deaths for 2024.
- An ICMR study projects the annual burden rising to about 2.7 million cases by 2050.
Different methods give different numbers, so quote the source with the figure.

## 2. Found late
- A pooled analysis of 58 hospital-based registries (2012 to 2016, JCO Global Oncology) found that fewer than a third of patients with common cancers presented at an early stage; most breast, cervix, head and neck and stomach cancers were locally advanced, and lung cancer mostly metastatic.
- In a Trivandrum district population-based study, the advanced-stage share was 37% for breast, 39% for cervix, 67% for oral cavity and 88% for lung cancer, and was higher among people with less education.
- Survey figures of "60% or more at stage III or IV" are widely quoted, but some come from a non-peer-reviewed survey or a secondary report of an academy paper.

## 3. Concentrated in a few places
- One review notes that although over half of India's population lives in central and eastern states, more than 60% of radiotherapy facilities are in the west and south.
- In Karnataka, nearly half of cancer care centres are concentrated around the capital, and many patients must travel over 8 hours for care.
- Commentators describe specialised cancer centres and oncologists as clustered in big cities and affluent areas. Exact ratios in the sources are weak, so avoid quoting them.
- In Kerala, the state has established public cancer centres, the Regional Cancer Centre in Thiruvananthapuram (1981) and the Malabar Cancer Centre in Thalassery (2001), both described as providing comprehensive cancer care. The question for Kerala is not whether a comprehensive centre exists, but how far that care reaches people close to home.

## 4. Lost after treatment
- In a Chennai registry study, even after active tracing, the survival status of 4% to 38% of patients (depending on cancer site) remained unknown.
- A South India hospital study of cervical cancer reported 34% of 690 patients lost to follow-up after completing planned treatment. A Kerala tertiary-centre study found few patients on regular follow-up.
- The reasons given are the cost of repeated travel to a centre, and low awareness.

## 5. What the Karkinos conversation adds
Dr. Kuriakose describes the problem from inside a platform built to fix it. In his words, Karkinos creates distributed cancer care: a platform from screening and diagnosis through treatment and follow-up, taking care out of the four walls of the hospital, with the community as a cancer centre. He says it has served about 3 million patients (his figure).
- **The bottleneck:** tertiary cancer centres were slow to adopt standardised care and have little incentive to send patients back to community cancer clinics for follow-up closer to home. This matches the follow-up gap above.
- **The bridges:** digital health in primary clinics, and adoption of ABDM in hospitals, so that a patient's record can move with them.
- **The vision:** a comprehensive cancer centre at the heart of a network of community cancer clinics.

## 6. Medinn's reading
Cancer care in India is not short of centres of excellence. It is short of the links between them and the community: patients who do not know which centre is right for them, referrals that flow in but not back out, and records that do not travel. Follow-up is always the toughest part, because it needs every one of those links to work at once.
Questions worth putting to the system (not conclusions): who has the incentive to send a patient back to a community clinic? What would a standard follow-up pathway look like, and who audits it? How does a patient find the right centre? What would make a primary clinic confident to follow a patient treated at a tertiary centre?
